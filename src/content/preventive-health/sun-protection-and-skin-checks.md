One in five Americans will develop skin cancer by age 70. Most cases are basal and squamous cell carcinomas, which are rarely fatal but are cut out of faces and hands at a rate of several million a year. About 100,000 a year are melanoma, which kills roughly 8,000. Nearly all of it is driven by ultraviolet exposure, and nearly all of it is either preventable or curable when found early.

## What ultraviolet does

Sunlight carries UVA, which penetrates deep, ages the skin, and contributes to cancer, and UVB, which burns and causes most of the DNA damage that leads to cancer. Both are present on cloudy days; clouds block about 20 percent. Both reflect off water, sand, and snow. Neither is blocked by ordinary window glass in the case of UVA.

Damage accumulates. The risk from a single blistering sunburn in childhood roughly doubles the lifetime risk of melanoma. Tanning is damage, not protection; a tan provides the equivalent of about SPF 3.

## Sunscreen

The trial evidence is from Australia, where a randomized study in Queensland assigned adults to daily sunscreen or discretionary use for four and a half years. Ten years on, the daily group had half the rate of melanoma and 40 percent fewer squamous cell carcinomas. It remains the strongest single piece of evidence for sunscreen.

What the label means:

**SPF** measures protection against UVB. SPF 30 blocks about 97 percent; SPF 50 about 98 percent. The difference is small, and above 50 is mostly marketing. SPF 30 or higher, applied properly, is the standard recommendation.

**Broad spectrum** means it also blocks UVA. Without those words, it may not.

**Water resistant** means it holds for 40 or 80 minutes of swimming or sweating, as stated. No sunscreen is waterproof.

**Mineral versus chemical.** Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide sit on the skin and reflect light. Chemical filters absorb it. Both work. Mineral filters suit sensitive skin and are the choice for infants. The FDA has asked for more safety data on several chemical filters; it has not found them unsafe, and dermatology bodies continue to recommend either type over no sunscreen.

The way people fail with sunscreen is quantity and timing. The tested dose is about 2 milligrams per square centimeter, which for a full body is about 30 milliliters, a shot glass. Most people apply a quarter to a half of that, which reduces SPF 30 to something like SPF 8. Apply 15 minutes before exposure and again every two hours, or after swimming or heavy sweating.

## Beyond sunscreen

Sunscreen is the last line, not the first. Shade between 10 am and 4 pm, when UV is strongest, does more. A wide brimmed hat protects the face, ears, and neck, where most skin cancers appear. Long sleeves in tightly woven or UPF rated fabric protect better than any lotion. Sunglasses with UV protection prevent cataracts and cancers of the eyelid. Tanning beds should be avoided entirely; use before 35 raises melanoma risk by about 75 percent.

The UV index, reported in most weather apps, is the simplest guide. Protection is advised at 3 and above, which in most of the United States means most daylight hours from spring to autumn.

## Vitamin D

The concern that sun protection causes vitamin D deficiency is largely unfounded. Sunscreen as actually applied still lets enough UVB through for synthesis, and the Australian trial found no difference in vitamin D between groups. People who avoid the sun entirely, or who live at high latitudes in winter, can take a supplement, which is cheaper than a skin cancer.

## The monthly self check

Melanoma found early, before it has grown deeper than about a millimeter, is cured by removal in nearly every case. Found late, it is one of the more dangerous cancers. The difference is usually who looked.

Once a month, in good light, with a mirror for the back, look at all of your skin, including scalp, between toes, soles, and under nails. What you are looking for is anything new, anything changing, and anything that does not look like its neighbors. The standard checklist for a mole is ABCDE:

- **A**symmetry: one half unlike the other
- **B**order: ragged, blurred, or notched
- **C**olor: more than one shade, or black, red, white, or blue
- **D**iameter: larger than about 6 millimeters, a pencil eraser
- **E**volving: changing in size, shape, color, or surface, or starting to itch or bleed

The E is the most important. A spot that is changing warrants a visit whatever it looks like. So does the "ugly duckling," a mole that looks different from all the others, and any sore that does not heal in a month.

Basal cell carcinomas often look like a pearly bump, a pink patch, or a sore that heals and reopens. Squamous cell carcinomas look like a scaly red patch, a firm bump, or a wart that grows. Both appear mostly on sun exposed skin.

## Professional checks

There is no consensus on routine dermatologist screening for average risk adults; the US Preventive Services Task Force finds the evidence insufficient either way. Annual professional checks are advised for people with a personal or family history of melanoma, many moles or unusual moles, fair skin that burns easily, a history of severe sunburns, or a weakened immune system. Everyone else should see someone when the self check finds something.
