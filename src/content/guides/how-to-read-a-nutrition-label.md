The Nutrition Facts panel has been on packaged food in the United States since 1994 and was redesigned in 2016. It contains more than a dozen figures. Most shopping decisions turn on four of them, and one of the most prominent is the least useful. Here is how to read it in the time it takes to pick something off a shelf.

## Start with the serving

The top of the panel states a serving size and how many servings the package contains. Every number below is per serving. Since 2016, serving sizes are meant to reflect what people actually eat, so a 20 ounce bottle of soda is one serving, not two and a half. But a bag of chips is still several servings, and a "share size" candy bar is two.

Check whether the serving matches what you will eat. If you will eat the bag, multiply everything by the servings per container. This single step catches more misjudgments than any other.

## The four lines that decide most choices

**Calories.** A rough guide to energy density. Useful for comparing two similar products. Not useful for deciding whether a food is good; 200 calories of almonds and 200 calories of gummy candy are not the same meal.

**Added sugars.** This line, introduced in the redesign, separates sugar added during manufacturing from sugar naturally present in fruit or milk. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans cap added sugar at 10 percent of calories, about 50 grams a day on a 2,000 calorie diet. The American Heart Association suggests half that. A single sweetened drink can hit the lower target on its own. Of every figure on the label, this is the one most worth a habit of checking.

**Fiber.** Most adults get about half the recommended 25 to 38 grams a day. A packaged grain product with 3 or more grams per serving is a reasonable choice; one with under 1 gram is refined regardless of what the front says. Fiber is also the quickest way to tell whether "whole grain" on the front means anything.

**Sodium.** The target is under 2,300 milligrams a day, and most people get about 3,400, nearly all of it from packaged and restaurant food rather than the salt shaker. A serving with over 20 percent of the daily value is a high sodium food. Bread, soup, sauces, and deli meat are where it hides. For anyone tracking [blood pressure](/preventive-health/how-to-take-blood-pressure-at-home/), this line matters more than the fat lines.

## The two that mislead

**Total fat.** Prominent at the top because the label was designed in the low fat era. Total fat says little on its own. Nuts, olive oil, and fish are high in fat and among the best foods available. The subline that matters is saturated fat, where the guideline is under 10 percent of calories, and even that is a coarser signal than the overall pattern of what you eat.

**Percent daily value.** The right column looks precise and is based on a 2,000 calorie diet that fits few individuals. It is useful only as a rough sort: 5 percent or less is low, 20 percent or more is high. Do not add them up.

## The ingredient list as tiebreaker

Ingredients are listed by weight, heaviest first. Three quick reads:

- **What is first?** If sugar, a syrup, or a refined flour leads, that is what the product mostly is.
- **How many names for sugar?** Cane sugar, corn syrup, dextrose, maltose, fruit juice concentrate, and brown rice syrup can appear separately, each far down the list, while together they would be first. Count them.
- **How long is it?** A short list of things you could buy yourself is a safe sign. A long list of extracted starches, isolated proteins, emulsifiers, and flavorings is the signature of [ultra processed food](/articles/ultra-processed-food-what-the-evidence-says/). Length is not a verdict, but it is a useful sort.

## Front of package claims

"Natural" has no regulated meaning for most foods. "Made with whole grains" can mean a small fraction. "Light" may refer to color or flavor. "No added sugar" allows concentrated fruit juice. "Reduced sodium" means 25 percent less than the regular version, which can still be a lot. Treat the front as advertising and the panel as data.

## The thirty second routine

Look at the serving size and adjust. Read added sugars, fiber, and sodium. Glance at the first three ingredients and count the sugars. Ignore the rest unless you have a specific reason to care about it, such as a clinician's advice on saturated fat or potassium.

That routine, applied to the ten or fifteen packaged products a household buys repeatedly, changes the diet more than any single meal decision. Most people find that two or three habitual purchases account for most of their added sugar and sodium, and swapping those is the whole intervention. As with most things Ribbon Checkup covers, it is not the perfect choice that matters. It is the default one.
