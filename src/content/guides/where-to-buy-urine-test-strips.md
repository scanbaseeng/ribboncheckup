Urine test strips are sold in pharmacies, big box stores, medical supply sites, and every large online marketplace. Prices vary by a factor of five for the same chemistry. Quality varies more. This guide covers where to buy and what the label terms mean.

## The label terms

**FDA cleared.** The maker submitted the product to the FDA and received clearance to market it for a stated use. For urine strips, this typically means the strip was shown to perform comparably to a product already on the market. Most reputable home strips are cleared. The listing or the box should say so.

**OTC or over the counter.** Cleared for use by a consumer at home without a prescription. This is the phrase that matters for home use. A strip cleared for professional use only was tested with trained readers, not with the general public.

**CLIA waived.** A separate classification for tests simple enough to run outside a formal lab. It applies to clinics and pharmacies, not to home users. A CLIA waived strip is a good sign of quality, but the term is not required for home sale.

**For in vitro diagnostic use.** Standard language meaning the product tests a sample outside the body. It is a minimum, not a mark of quality.

**Research use only.** The product has not been cleared for diagnosis of anything. Some are fine. Some are not. The seller is not making any claim, and neither should you.

**Veterinary use.** Made for animal urine. The pads are often identical to human strips. The quality control may not be.

## Pharmacies and big box stores

Reliable. The stock is fresh, the storage is controlled, and the products are cleared for home use. Selection is narrow. You will find UTI tests, ketone strips, and glucose strips. You will rarely find a 10 parameter dipstick or a uACR kit on the shelf. Prices are the highest.

Buy here for: UTI tests when you need one today. Ketone strips for a first month.

## Medical supply sites

These sites sell to clinics and also to consumers. They carry the full range, including professional 10 parameter strips in bottles of 100, microalbumin strips, and uACR kits. Stock turns fast, so expiration dates are long. Prices are low per strip.

Buy here for: 10 parameter dipsticks. Kidney tests. Anything you will use regularly.

## Online marketplaces

The widest selection and the lowest prices. Also the highest share of problems. The issues, in order of frequency:

- **Short dated stock.** Strips with a few months left, sold without the date shown.
- **Poor storage.** Third party sellers keep stock in warehouses without climate control. Strips arrive with pads already discolored.
- **Repackaging.** Strips from a bulk bottle moved into a new container without a desiccant.
- **Mislabeled intended use.** Research use or veterinary strips listed in the human health category.
- **Chart quality.** Some low cost brands print charts with poor color separation.

None of this means avoid marketplaces. It means read the listing.

## How to read a marketplace listing

1. Does the listing show the expiration date, or say "at least 12 months"? If not, ask the seller before buying.
2. Does it show the color chart clearly in a product photo? Zoom in. Can you tell trace from 1+?
3. Does the listing say "OTC" or "for home use"? Or does it say "professional" or "research"?
4. Is the seller the manufacturer, a medical supplier, or an unknown storefront? Prefer the first two.
5. Do the reviews complain about pads that are discolored on arrival? That is storage, and it will happen to you.
6. Is the count you are buying one you will use within 90 days of opening? If not, buy fewer.

## Storage after purchase

This matters more than where you bought them. Most "bad strips" were good strips stored badly.

- Keep strips in the original bottle with the desiccant. Do not transfer them.
- Close the cap right after taking a strip out. Seconds count in humid air.
- Store at room temperature, out of direct light. Not in the bathroom. Not in a car.
- Do not refrigerate unless the label says to. Condensation when you open a cold bottle is worse than warm storage.
- Write the opening date on the bottle. Discard at 90 days or on the printed expiration, whichever comes first.
- Individually wrapped strips avoid all of this. They cost more. For a kit you use a few times a year, they are the better buy.

## Price expectations

A 10 parameter dipstick from a medical supplier costs a fraction of a pharmacy UTI test per strip. A uACR home strip costs several times a standard dipstick. A mail in uACR lab kit costs the most. Blood ketone strips cost many times what urine ketone strips do. The gap in each case buys either convenience or a better answer. Decide which you are paying for.
