A strength program needs to do three things: load the major movement patterns, get harder over time, and be simple enough that you actually do it twice a week for years. This one is built for that. It uses bodyweight to start and a pair of dumbbells or a filled backpack once bodyweight stops being enough.

If you want the reasons before the method, read [strength training after 40](/articles/strength-training-after-40/). If you just want to start, here it is.

## The six movements

Each one covers a pattern the body uses every day. Do them in this order.

**1. Sit to stand (squat pattern).** Stand in front of a chair, feet shoulder width. Lower until you touch the seat, without sitting fully, then stand. Keep your weight in your heels and your chest up. Harder: do not touch the chair. Harder still: hold a weight at your chest.

**2. Hip hinge (deadlift pattern).** Stand with a soft bend in the knees. Push your hips back as if closing a car door with them, letting your torso tip forward with a flat back, until you feel your hamstrings stretch. Stand back up by driving the hips forward. Harder: hold a weight in each hand and lower it toward your shins.

**3. Push up (push pattern).** Hands under shoulders, body in a straight line. Lower your chest to a fist's height from the floor, then push back. Easier: hands on a countertop or a wall. Harder: feet on a step.

**4. Row (pull pattern).** Hold a weight in one hand, brace the other on a chair, hinge forward with a flat back, and pull the weight to your hip. Lower slowly. Without weights: lie under a sturdy table, grip the edge, and pull your chest to it.

**5. Split squat (single leg pattern).** One foot forward, one back, as in a long step. Lower the back knee toward the floor, then stand. This is the one that fixes balance. Harder: hold weights, or put the back foot on a step.

**6. Carry.** Pick up something heavy in each hand and walk 20 to 30 steps with good posture. Grocery bags, dumbbells, jugs of water. Nothing trains the trunk and grip better.

## The sessions

Two per week, at least two days apart. Each takes 25 to 35 minutes.

Warm up for 3 minutes: march in place, arm circles, a few slow bodyweight squats.

Then for each of the six movements: two sets in the first month, three sets after that. Each set is 8 to 12 repetitions, or as many as you can with good form if you cannot reach 8. The carry is two walks. Rest 60 to 90 seconds between sets.

The rule that makes it a program rather than a workout: **the last two repetitions of each set should be hard.** If you can do 12 easily, make the movement harder next time, using the progressions above or adding weight. If you cannot do 8, make it easier. That is all progressive overload means, and it is the only thing that produces results.

## The first four weeks

Expect soreness for two or three days after the first two sessions. It fades and does not come back at that intensity. Expect the numbers to move quickly: the sit to stand that felt like work in week one is warm up by week four. That early progress is the nervous system learning to use the muscle you already have. Visible change in muscle comes later, around week eight to twelve, and continues for years.

Do not add days. Two sessions a week is the dose with the evidence behind it. Three is fine once the habit is solid. More than that is for people with other goals.

## Equipment, when you want it

A pair of adjustable dumbbells, 2 to 20 kilograms each, covers this program for at least a year. A backpack filled with books is a free substitute for the squat, hinge, and split squat. Resistance bands make the row easier to set up. None of it is needed to start.

## Tracking progress

Keep a log. Date, movement, sets, repetitions, and weight or variation. The log is what tells you when to progress and the only way to see how far you have come. Once a month, do the three tests from the [strength training article](/articles/strength-training-after-40/): timed five sit to stands, maximum push ups, and heaviest carry. Write those down too.

## Pain versus effort

Effort feels like a burning, tiring muscle that recovers within a minute. Pain is sharp, located in a joint, or gets worse from one repetition to the next. Effort is the point. Pain is a signal to stop that movement, use an easier variation next time, and see someone if it persists for more than a week.

## Fitting it with everything else

This program pairs with walking or running on the other days. If you do both, put the strength sessions on days when you do not run, or after an easy run, not before a long one. Eat protein at each meal; the article on training after 40 has the numbers. And sleep, because muscle is built between sessions, not during them. Ribbon Checkup's piece on [how much sleep adults need](/articles/how-much-sleep-adults-actually-need/) explains why that is not a platitude.
