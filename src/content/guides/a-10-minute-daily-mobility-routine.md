Most adults sit for nine or more hours a day. Hips stay bent, shoulders round forward, the upper back stiffens into the shape of a laptop screen, and the ankles do nothing. Joints keep the range they use and lose the range they do not. That, more than age, is why getting off the floor becomes a project in your 50s.

The fix is not intensive. Ten minutes a day of taking each major joint through its full range, with control, maintains and gradually restores mobility in almost everyone. It also does something static stretching does not: it teaches the nervous system to control the new range, which is what makes it usable.

## Mobility versus flexibility

Flexibility is how far a joint can be pushed passively. Mobility is how far you can move it yourself, with control. A person can touch their toes and still have a hip that will not squat. Static stretching, holding a position for 30 seconds or more, improves the first. Controlled movement through range improves the second, and the evidence favors it for daily function, for warming up, and for reducing the stiffness that comes from sitting. Static stretching keeps its place after exercise and for specific tight spots.

## The routine

Move slowly. Breathe. Go to the edge of comfortable range, not past it. Each movement gets about a minute.

**1. Cat cow (spine).** On hands and knees. Round the whole back toward the ceiling, tucking the chin, then reverse, lifting the chest and tailbone. Ten slow cycles. This wakes up every segment of the spine.

**2. Thoracic rotation (upper back).** Still on hands and knees, put one hand behind your head. Rotate that elbow down toward the opposite wrist, then up toward the ceiling, following it with your eyes. Eight each side. The upper back is where desk posture does its damage and this is the movement that reverses it.

**3. Hip circles (hips).** On hands and knees, lift one knee slightly off the floor and draw the largest slow circle you can with it, in both directions. Five each way, each side.

**4. Deep squat hold (hips, ankles, knees).** Feet slightly wider than shoulders, lower into the deepest squat you can with heels down. Hold onto a door frame or table if needed. Stay for 30 to 60 seconds, shifting weight gently side to side. This position is the single best test and treatment for lower body stiffness.

**5. Hip flexor and hamstring flow.** Step into a long lunge with the back knee on the floor. Push the hips forward and lift the arm on the back leg side overhead. Then rock back, straightening the front leg and folding forward over it. Alternate for six cycles, then switch sides. This addresses the two muscles that sitting shortens most.

**6. Shoulder circles with a stick (shoulders).** Hold a broomstick or towel wide with both hands. Keeping the arms straight, lift it from your thighs, over your head, and as far behind you as you can, then back. Narrow the grip as it gets easier. Ten slow passes.

**7. Ankle rocks (ankles).** In a half kneeling position, front foot flat, drive the front knee forward over the toes as far as it goes without the heel lifting. Ten each side. Ankle range is what determines whether you can squat, climb stairs, and walk downhill comfortably.

**8. Standing reach and fold (whole body).** Reach both arms overhead, rise onto your toes, then fold forward and hang, letting the head and arms relax. Five slow cycles. Finish here.

## When to do it

The routine works best attached to something fixed. First thing in the morning is popular and has one drawback: the spine is stiffest then, so go gently on the first two movements. After a walk is ideal because everything is warm. Before a strength session it doubles as a warm up. The wrong time is the one you skip, so pick the anchor that actually holds.

For people who sit all day, a second smaller dose helps: stand up every 30 to 45 minutes, do five squats or a minute of thoracic rotation, sit back down. The interruption itself has measurable effects on blood sugar and blood pressure, separate from the movement.

## What to expect

Most people feel looser after a single session and notice a lasting change in three to four weeks, most obviously in the deep squat and in how the upper back feels at the end of a work day. Range keeps improving for months. It does not need to be pushed. Consistency does the work.

Discomfort at the end of a range is expected. Sharp pain, pain in a joint rather than a muscle, or numbness and tingling are not; skip that movement and see someone if it persists.
