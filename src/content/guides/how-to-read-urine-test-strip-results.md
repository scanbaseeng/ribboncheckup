The strip is rarely the problem. The reading is. A dipstick is a timed chemical reaction read by eye against a printed chart. Every step has a way to go wrong. Here is the full procedure, then the mistakes we see most.

## Before you start

- Check the expiration date on the bottle.
- Read the timing chart on the label. Write down the read time for each pad if there are several.
- Find a clean, dry cup. Do not reuse a cup that held soap or bleach.
- Have a timer ready. A phone works.
- Find good light. Daylight near a window is best. Warm indoor light shifts colors.

## Collection

Use first morning urine when you can. It is the most concentrated and gives the strongest readings. It also meets the four hour bladder time that the nitrite pad needs.

Collect midstream. Start urinating into the toilet, then move the cup into the stream for a second or two, then finish in the toilet. The first part of the stream washes skin bacteria and cells into the sample. Skipping this step is the leading cause of false leukocyte positives.

Test within a few minutes. Urine left standing changes. Bacteria multiply. pH rises. Bilirubin breaks down in light.

## Dipping

1. Take one strip out. Close the bottle immediately, with the desiccant inside.
2. Do not touch the pads.
3. Dip the strip so every pad is wet. One second is enough.
4. Pull it out and drag the edge along the cup rim to shed excess urine.
5. Hold the strip level, or lay it flat on a paper towel. Do not hold it upright. Urine runs from one pad into the next and mixes the chemistry.
6. Start the timer the moment the strip leaves the urine.

## Reading

Read each pad at the time printed for it. Not before. Not after. Hold the strip next to the chart with the pads lined up. Match the closest color block.

The order on the strip usually matches the order on the chart, but confirm it. Some brands print the chart reversed relative to the strip.

For pads that use "any color change means positive," such as nitrite, do not wait for the darkest block. Faint pink is positive.

Write down the result immediately. Colors keep changing.

## The seven mistakes

### 1. Reading late

The most common error. Every pad keeps developing after its window. A strip read at three minutes shows glucose, protein, and ketones darker than their true value. Set the timer.

### 2. Reading early

The leukocyte pad takes up to two minutes. Reading it at 60 seconds with the other pads gives a false negative on the one pad that matters for infection.

### 3. Holding the strip vertically

Urine drains down the strip and pools in the lowest pad. Reagents wash between pads. The pH pad next to the protein pad is the classic victim. Lay it flat.

### 4. Leaving the strip in the cup

Extra urine washes reagents out of the pads. Colors come out weak. Dip and remove.

### 5. Bad light

Yellow bulbs make everything look more yellow. That shifts glucose and ketone readings. Use daylight or a bright white light.

### 6. Reading in the bathroom

Strips absorb moisture from the air. A bottle opened repeatedly in a humid room loses sensitivity within weeks. Store the bottle somewhere dry and bring one strip to the bathroom.

### 7. Trusting one reading

A single strip is a snapshot. Hydration, time of day, food, exercise, and menstruation all move the results. Anything unexpected should be repeated on another morning before you act on it.

## When the strip is discolored before you use it

If a fresh strip has pads that are already tan, brown, or blotchy, the bottle has been exposed to moisture or light. Do not use it. Compare a new strip to the "negative" blocks on the chart. If they do not match, discard the bottle.

## Recording results

Keep a simple log with date, time, whether it was first morning urine, and each pad's value. Trends mean more than single points. A clinician can do a lot with three weeks of morning readings and nothing with one afternoon strip.
