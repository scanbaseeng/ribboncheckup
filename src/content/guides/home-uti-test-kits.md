Home UTI tests are the best selling urine test kit in the United States. They are also the most misread. The kits are simple. The biology is not. Here is what you are actually buying.

## What is on the strip

A home UTI test has two pads. Some add a third for blood.

**Nitrite.** Common urinary bacteria, especially E. coli, convert nitrate from food into nitrite. Urine normally contains no nitrite. A pink pad means bacteria are present and have had time to work. This pad is specific. When it is positive, an infection is very likely.

**Leukocyte esterase.** White blood cells release this enzyme. Their presence means the body is fighting something in the urinary tract. The pad is sensitive but less specific. It turns positive in infection, and also in inflammation without infection, and with vaginal contamination.

The kits are designed to be read together. Both positive: strong signal. Nitrite positive alone: strong signal. Leukocytes positive alone: possible infection, possible something else. Both negative: infection less likely but not excluded.

## Why negatives are unreliable

The nitrite pad has two requirements that a lot of users never hear about.

First, the bacteria must be a nitrate reducer. E. coli, Klebsiella, and Proteus qualify. Enterococcus and Staphylococcus saprophyticus do not. Those two cause a meaningful share of UTIs, particularly in younger women. With those organisms the nitrite pad stays negative no matter how bad the infection is.

Second, the urine must sit in the bladder long enough for the conversion to happen. The usual figure is four hours. If you are drinking a lot of water and urinating every hour, the pad may never turn. First morning urine solves this. Test it before you drink anything.

Published studies put the nitrite pad's sensitivity around 50 percent. That means it misses about half of real infections. Its specificity is above 90 percent, which is why a positive is trustworthy. Leukocyte esterase runs the other way, with higher sensitivity and lower specificity. Combined, the two pads catch most infections but still miss some.

## How to test correctly

1. Use first morning urine, or urine that has been in the bladder at least four hours.
2. Collect midstream. Start urinating, then move the cup into the stream. The first part flushes out contaminants from the skin.
3. Dip the strip briefly and pull it out. Do not leave it in the cup. Extra urine dilutes the reagents.
4. Tap the edge on the cup rim to remove excess.
5. Lay it flat. Start a timer.
6. Read nitrite at the time on the label, usually 30 to 60 seconds. Read leukocytes at 60 to 120 seconds.
7. Compare to the chart in good light. Any pink on the nitrite pad counts as positive. Do not wait for it to match the darkest block.

Do not read the strip after the window closes. Both pads keep darkening on their own.

## Symptoms that mean skip the kit

A home kit is for the situation where you have mild symptoms and want to know whether to bother a clinician. It is not for these:

- Fever or chills
- Pain in the back or side, below the ribs
- Visible blood in the urine
- Nausea or vomiting
- Symptoms in someone who is pregnant
- Symptoms in a man, a child, or anyone with a catheter
- Symptoms that came back within a few weeks of finishing antibiotics

Each of those points to a situation where a delay matters. The strip result changes nothing. Call.

## What a positive result gets you

A positive home test does not get you antibiotics by itself. Most clinicians will still want a urine sample, and many will send it for culture to identify the organism and pick the right drug. What the home test does is shorten the conversation. "I have symptoms and my nitrite pad is positive" is a clear message. Many telehealth services will prescribe on that basis for uncomplicated cases in adult women.

A negative home test with ongoing symptoms should also go to a clinician. Remember the miss rate.

## Buying notes

- Two pad kits are enough. The blood pad on three pad kits adds little.
- Kits sold in sealed foil pouches, one strip per pouch, keep better than bottles. They cost more per strip. For occasional use, the pouches are worth it.
- Check the expiration date. A stale leukocyte pad reads weak.
- Some kits include a chart that reads "negative, trace, small, moderate, large." Others read "negative, positive." Either is fine. The graded chart is not more accurate. It is just more colors to match.
- Kits sold for general "urinary health" without nitrite and leukocyte pads are not UTI tests. Read the pad list.
