"Ultra processed" has become the health word of the decade, applied to everything from soda to sliced bread to protein bars. The claim underneath it is that the degree of industrial processing, separate from calories, sugar, salt, or fat, is what makes modern food harmful. That claim has some real evidence behind it and a lot of noise around it.

## What the term means

The NOVA classification, developed at the University of São Paulo in 2009, sorts food into four groups by processing rather than nutrients.

1. **Unprocessed or minimally processed.** Vegetables, fruit, eggs, milk, meat, grains, nuts. Washing, freezing, drying, and pasteurizing keep a food in this group.
2. **Processed culinary ingredients.** Oils, butter, sugar, salt, flour.
3. **Processed foods.** Group 1 foods with group 2 ingredients added: canned vegetables, cheese, fresh bread, cured meat.
4. **Ultra processed foods.** Industrial formulations made mostly from substances extracted from food, plus additives that a home kitchen does not use: emulsifiers, hydrolyzed proteins, modified starches, flavorings, colorings. Soft drinks, packaged snacks, most breakfast cereals, instant noodles, reconstituted meat products, many ready meals, and mass produced packaged bread.

In the United States, group 4 supplies close to 60 percent of adult calories, and about two thirds of what children eat.

## The evidence that holds up

Dozens of cohort studies link higher ultra processed intake to higher rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, heart disease, depression, and death. A 2024 umbrella review in the BMJ pooled 45 of them and found consistent associations, strongest for cardiovascular death, diabetes, anxiety, and all cause mortality.

Cohort studies cannot separate the processing from the people. Those who eat the most ultra processed food also tend to have lower incomes, smoke more, and move less, and the statistical adjustments for that are never complete.

The study that moves the argument is a 2019 trial at the US National Institutes of Health. Twenty adults lived on a research ward for four weeks. For two weeks they were offered an ultra processed diet, for two an unprocessed one, in random order, with meals matched for calories, sugar, fat, fiber, and salt presented. They could eat as much or as little as they wanted. On the ultra processed diet they ate about 500 more calories a day and gained about a kilogram in two weeks. On the unprocessed diet they lost the same amount. The foods were matched on the nutrients that usually get the blame, and the difference still appeared.

It is a small study, but it is the kind of study that can show cause, and the effect was large. Follow up work has pointed to faster eating rate, higher energy density, and softer texture as likely reasons people eat more of these foods before feeling full.

## Where the evidence is weak

The category is too broad to be a single thing. Whole grain packaged bread and a can of energy drink are both group 4. Studies that split the category find most of the harm concentrated in sugary drinks and processed meat, with much weaker or no association for packaged whole grain bread, breakfast cereal, or yogurt. Some group 4 foods, like fortified cereals or plant based milks, may improve diets that are otherwise short of nutrients.

Additives get blamed individually, and for a few, emulsifiers and some sweeteners, there is early evidence of effects on the gut microbiome. For most, there is not. The mechanism story is still being written.

And the framing invites a purity mindset that the data does not support. The cohort studies compare the top fifth of intake to the bottom fifth. There is no evidence that the occasional packaged snack does anything measurable.

## How to use this without a rulebook

The practical advice is close to what nutrition science already said, with one addition.

Eat mostly food that looks like food: vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, eggs, fish, dairy, meat in moderation. That covers groups 1 to 3 and is the basis of every diet with good long term evidence, including the [Mediterranean pattern](/health-explained/the-mediterranean-diet-explained/).

Within group 4, target the two with the strongest evidence: sugar sweetened drinks and processed meat. Cutting those does more than anything else on the list.

Then use the ingredient list. A product with a short list of ingredients you could buy yourself is a safe bet whatever its NOVA group. A long list of extracted substances and additives is the signature of the category. The [nutrition label guide](/guides/how-to-read-a-nutrition-label/) covers how to read one quickly.

Notice speed. The NIH trial's most striking finding was that people ate ultra processed meals faster. Slowing down, and eating foods that require chewing, is one of the few interventions that targets the mechanism directly.

## Why this matters for the numbers you track

High intake of the foods at the top of the list shows up in numbers Ribbon Checkup covers elsewhere: fasting glucose and [A1C](/preventive-health/diabetes-screening-a1c-and-fasting-glucose/), triglycerides on a [cholesterol panel](/health-explained/cholesterol-panel-explained/), blood pressure, and waist circumference. If those are drifting, the composition of the grocery cart is the first place to look, and it is easier to change than most people expect once the target is drinks and processed meat rather than everything in a package.
